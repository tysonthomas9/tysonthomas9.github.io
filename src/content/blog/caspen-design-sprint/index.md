A few months ago a few startups from our co-working space were picked for a [Google Design Sprint workshop](/blog/google-design-sprint/) in Bangalore. It was two days of moving fast through six stages, from understanding a problem to testing a solution with real users. Back at our desks, we tried it on our own work.

So we ran a sprint of our own. We didn't point it at a feature or a screen. We pointed it at the question underneath everything else: who is our product for?

This post is about that sprint: why we chose that question, how we went looking for answers, and where it has left us.

## The question we couldn't answer

Our startup is called Caspen. We work on design and analysis tools for mechanical engineers, and simulation is at the heart of it. Computational fluid dynamics, or CFD, lets you test how air or liquid moves around a design on a computer before anything is built: the airflow in aerodynamics work, or the flow inside industrial equipment. We're building on OpenFOAM, the open-source CFD toolkit, along with other tools, and running it in the cloud, for both industry and students.

The trouble was that the same tool could serve very different people. Students learning mechanical engineering could use it to practise. Companies could use it for real design work. And a platform where engineers prove their skills could help companies hire them. Each of these is a different product with a different customer.

That's the question we took into the sprint.

## Two months, not two days

The workshop squeezed the whole process into two days. Ours ran for two months. It was a team effort.

The first stage of the sprint is Understand, and for us that meant going out and asking. Before anyone talked to anyone, we wrote down who we wanted to hear from and what we wanted to ask them.

## Talking to everyone we might serve

We split our possible customers into four groups:

- **Students.** Mechanical engineering students, roughly 18 to 25.
- **Companies.** Firms in product design and engineering services that hire design and analysis engineers.
- **Colleges.** The faculty who run the labs, and the placement departments who help students find jobs.
- **Working engineers.** Analysts already in the industry.

For each group we wrote two sets of questions, because we were weighing two ways Caspen could help.

**As a learning partner.** Do students feel they lack the skills industry wants? Is it hard to apply what they learn in class? How useful are their labs, and would they take a course online? For companies: how do they train new hires, how long does it take, and what costs and pain points come with it? For colleges: do they use online tools, and do they have the people and equipment to teach analysis well in the lab? For working engineers: how often do they need to learn something new, and do they study in their own time?

**As a recruitment partner.** Do students rely only on campus placements, and how do they know how good they really are, beyond lab exams? Would they take an online test or a design challenge to show what they can do? For companies: do new hires usually fit the job, and how long does it take to find and assess them? For placement departments: would they try a new way of placing students, such as having them assessed through design challenges? For working engineers: how do they look for a new job, and would they take on a challenge posted by a company to prove themselves?

Every conversation started the same way. We said who we were and that we were in the middle of a design sprint, and we promised to come back and show people what we built from their feedback.

We also wrote a user story to keep one person in view. A recruiter who hires mechanical engineers every year hears about a platform that finds the right candidates in a fraction of the time, and wants to see how it assesses them and what else it offers.

## Three roles on the table

By the end of the sprint we had three roles Caspen could play:

| | Learning | Recruitment | Simulation tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Who it serves** | Students, plus engineers who want to keep learning | Companies hiring engineers, and students and engineers looking for jobs | Companies and consultants doing real design work |
| **What it offers** | Practical skills and exposure to real industry projects | A way to prove skills through design challenges, and a faster way to find the right people | Design and analysis in the cloud, with cloud computing for the heavy analysis |

The simulation tool can be cut more than one way, too. Small and medium manufacturers know designing on a computer saves money, but many don't have the skills or machines to do it. Consultants and experts could do the work for them on a shared tool in the cloud. Large companies with design teams in different places could use the same tool to share models and results. Students could use it to work on industry-style projects with experts guiding them.

What all of these have in common is the engine underneath: a design and analysis tool in the cloud that people can work on together. The open question is who to build it for first.

## What's still rough

We haven't picked yet. The sprint didn't give us one answer. It gave us three clear options, each with its own customer, and we are still weighing them.

## What we'd tell someone starting out

Talk to every group you might serve. Students, companies, colleges and working engineers each told us something different. Ask each group about every role you're considering, not just the one you expect them to fit.

Next we plan to choose one role, and to go back to the people we interviewed and show them what we built from their feedback, as we promised.
